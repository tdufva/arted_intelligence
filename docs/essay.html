Drafted for a Studies in Art Education-style reading context

From pedagogical intelligence to more-than-human cognition: Re-reading the Studies corpus through AI research and Umwelt

Abstract

This essay argues that the concept of intelligence in Studies in Art Education is best understood not as a singular hierarchy but as a shifting ecology of cognition, creativity, perception, pedagogy, and relation. Drawing first on a 174-article local corpus from the journal, it shows that intelligence in the field remains strongly cognitive in vocabulary yet is more often defined as situated social practice, creative capacity, and meaning-making than as measurement alone. The essay then turns to a separate curated arXiv sample on intelligence in AI research, where intelligence is more strongly framed as machinic relation, benchmarked capability, explanation, and embodied systems. Finally, the essay uses Umwelt, especially through James Bridle's account of machine perception, to argue that the comparison compels a rethinking of intelligence as world-relative and more-than-human. For art education, this suggests a shift from treating AI as merely a tool or threat toward teaching intelligence as an ecological relation among humans, machines, materials, publics, and places.

Keywords: intelligence, art education, AI, Umwelt, James Bridle, cognition, ecology, more-than-human pedagogy

Intelligence in the Studies corpus

The local Studies in Art Education corpus suggests that intelligence in art education has never been reducible to one stable measure. The corpus opens with Eisner's (1962) effort to typologize creativity in the visual arts, and that opening already matters: from the beginning, intelligence appears in the journal as entangled with making, judgment, imagination, and educational formation rather than as a purely psychometric trait. Across the 174 articles in this dataset, cognitive and conceptual language remains the most common intelligence type (74.7%), yet the most frequent definition frames are situated social and cultural practice (61.5%), creative capacity (51.7%), and measurable faculty (49.4%). Taken together, those proportions indicate a field that never fully abandons cognition, but repeatedly reworks it through practice, imagination, and pedagogy.

The middle decades and early 2000s deepen that point. Burton et al. (2000) frame the arts as a site where transfer depends on cognitive capacities and dispositions but also on how those capacities are embedded in curricular experience. Duncum (2004) expands the discourse further by arguing that visual culture is fundamentally multimodal, making intelligence legible not only through sight but through layered sign systems and meaning practices. In the corpus coding, this broader orientation appears in the way intelligence is recognized: most often through developmental observation (60.3%) and artworks and making (55.7%), followed by language and reflection (48.3%) and participation and relation (45.4%). Intelligence in the journal is therefore not only something one possesses. It is something that emerges in making, interpretation, classroom action, and pedagogical encounter.

The location frames tell a similarly mixed story. Intelligence is located most often in the mind and concepts (68.4%), but it also appears in classrooms and pedagogical systems (57.5%), artworks and materials (56.9%), and relations and culture (39.7%). Subject positions reinforce this pedagogical ecology: children and students (84.5%), artists and makers (70.1%), and teachers and educators (67.2%) dominate the corpus, while machines and nonhumans appear in only 6.3% of articles. That distribution makes the journal's historical emphasis clear. Intelligence in this field is mostly imagined as human, developmental, social, and educational, even when it borrows the language of assessment or cognition.

At the same time, the corpus contains the seeds of a broader account. Graham (2007) locates art education within a critical pedagogy of place that binds social and ecological issues together, while Gradle (2007) describes place as relational, phenomenological, and culturally layered. More recent work makes the computational turn explicit without surrendering the pedagogical one. Sweeny (2022) treats digital visual culture as a site for critical pedagogy; Park (2023) argues for collaborative and symbiotic relations with AI beyond instrumental use; and Freedman (2025) links generative AI to tacit knowledge, new materialism, and visual culture. These later interventions do not simply import AI into art education. They pressure the earlier discourse of intelligence to account for distributed agency, media systems, and nonhuman participation.

Expanding the problem through arXiv

The separate arXiv sample makes a different cut through the concept of intelligence. In that curated 26-paper set, intelligence is overwhelmingly digital and computational (92.3%) and cognitive and conceptual (92.3%), while the strongest definition frame is technological and machinic relation (65.4%). Measurement remains central: measurable faculty appears in 50.0% of the sample, and canonical benchmark-oriented formulations such as Legg and Hutter's (2007) universal definition and Chollet's (2019) account of skill-acquisition efficiency still organize much of the field's self-understanding. From this angle, intelligence becomes visible as generalization, test performance, and scalable capability.

Yet the expanded arXiv set also complicates the most familiar story of AI as nothing but benchmark culture. Islam et al. (2021) show that intelligence in AI research must also become explainable, accountable, and socially legible. Zahedi and Kambhampati (2021) move the conversation toward human-AI symbiosis, where intelligence is no longer the property of a single system but the accomplishment of a collaborative arrangement. Casadei (2023) pushes this further by reframing intelligence as a collective and engineered emergent phenomenon rather than a solitary cognitive summit. Wang and Sun (2025), meanwhile, make embodiment central to general intelligence itself, arguing that environment, action, and physical grounding are constitutive rather than optional.

Set beside the Studies in Art Education corpus, the difference is not that one field cares about intelligence and the other about art. The difference is that arXiv research more readily abstracts intelligence into machinic capability, while Studies more often embeds intelligence in pedagogy, making, and situated relation. Even where both corpora meet around measurement, language, or participation, they mobilize those terms within different institutional imaginaries. The journal asks how intelligence is formed and recognized in educational worlds. The AI sample asks how intelligence can be formalized, scaled, explained, or embodied in technical systems.

Umwelt and the redistribution of intelligence

Umwelt reframes the comparison by asking a different question. Instead of asking where intelligence sits on a universal ladder, Umwelt asks what kind of world a being can sense, inhabit, and act within (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.). Bridle's (2022) use of Umwelt is especially productive here because it shifts the discussion of machine intelligence away from the fantasy of total capacity and toward the partial worlds that machines compose and fail to compose. The issue is not simply whether a machine is intelligent in the abstract; it is what world becomes available to that machine and what remains inaccessible or unshared.

This move makes the affinity between Bridle and the art-education corpus easier to see. Graham (2007) and Gradle (2007) had already positioned art education within place, relation, ecology, and embodied belonging. Duncum (2004) had already argued that meaning exceeds the merely visual and must be read across multimodal cultural forms. Park (2023) and Freedman (2025) extend those concerns into AI, where intelligence is no longer only human but redistributed across interfaces, systems, images, data, and tacit practices. What Umwelt adds is a sharper ontological claim: intelligence is plural not only because cultures value different abilities, but because perception itself is world-specific.

For art education, the implication is significant. The task is not simply to celebrate AI as the next intelligence frontier, nor to defend human creativity as an untouched refuge. The task is to reorganize inquiry around relations among human, machinic, material, and ecological forms of sensing and acting. A curriculum built on that premise would treat critique, making, and technological literacy as ways of crossing Umwelten: learning how a child, an image system, a public, or a landscape renders significance differently, and what ethical obligations emerge from those mismatched worlds. Read this way, the concept of intelligence in Studies in Art Education points beyond both classical developmentalism and contemporary benchmark culture. Its most durable contribution may be its insistence that intelligence is not exhausted by tests or tools. Intelligence is also a pedagogical, cultural, and ecological relation.

Argument spine

In the journal corpus, intelligence is plural, pedagogical, and materially mediated.

In the arXiv sample, intelligence is more strongly machinic, benchmarked, and computational, even when explanation and embodiment complicate that pattern.

Umwelt ties the two together by reframing intelligence as a situated world relation rather than a universal rank.

How to read the numbers

All percentages refer to the share of articles or papers inside a corpus. Because the coding is plural, categories are not mutually exclusive and do not sum to 100.
